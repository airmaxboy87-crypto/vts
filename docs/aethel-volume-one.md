# AETHEL
## Volume One: The Sound Beneath the Ice

*Before it became a legend, the end of the world happened to everyone differently.*

---

# Chapter One
## The Wrong Note
### Orun · 420 days before the Flood

The ice made three kinds of sound.

It whispered when snow crossed its skin. It groaned when the weight of winter pressed old air from old wounds. And, in the deepest part of night, it sang.

Eira Sen had been taught never to give the songs names. A named thing accumulated intentions. If she called one note grief and another warning, sooner or later she would hear only what she expected. So she sat in the Third Vault with her knees beneath a reindeer-wool blanket and recorded what came through the listening stone.

Low pulse. Seven breaths.

Silence.

Returning pulse. Nine breaths.

She pressed one hand to the basalt pillar. It descended through the floor, through eighty arm-lengths of frozen gravel, and into the blue body of the shelf. The stone was cold enough to hurt, though the vault lamps warmed the air and the braziers had been fed since sunset.

Eira counted again.

The first pulse had travelled from the western marker to the city in seven breaths. Its echo should have returned in eleven. It came in nine.

She lifted her stylus but did not mark the clay.

Above her, a drum began.

One soft impact, then another, gathering speed. Dust fell from the arched ceiling. Pell Orro’s winter concert had begun in the Hall of Names, six vaults overhead. Six hundred people would be standing barefoot upon the warmed floor while her father’s stone instruments carried music through their bones.

Eira waited for the rhythm to settle. The glacier’s returning pulse had not been part of the concert. She was almost certain.

Almost was an opening through which fear entered.

She reset the striker. It was a simple device: a suspended stone weight, a release cord and a copper plate set into the western marker half a day’s walk away. Pull the cord here and a lamp signal flashed from tower to tower. At the marker, the keeper released the weight. Its blow travelled through ice and stone. The vault received it. By comparing the outgoing note to its returning echoes, the listeners mapped what no eye could see.

Cavities. Rivers. Fractures. Pressure.

Eira pulled the cord.

The first tower lamp opened like a white eye. The second answered on the ridge. Then the third, barely visible through windblown snow.

She set two fingers against the listening stone.

Above, Pell’s drums stopped.

For one perfect moment Orun held its breath.

The strike arrived.

Eira counted.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven.

Silence entered the stone.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight—

The concert returned in a thunder of feet.

The pillar trembled beneath her palm. Deep tones crossed and tangled. She shut her eyes, trying to separate human rhythm from glacial echo, but the whole city had become an instrument. Six hundred heels struck stone together. Copper bells answered. People cried out as Pell opened the north resonator and made the floor seem to roll like summer thunder.

Eira snatched her hand away.

“Of course,” she said to the empty vault.

She always spoke when embarrassed, even alone. Objects were easier witnesses than people. The clay tablet waited without judgment.

She wrote: **Second trial spoiled by surface vibration.**

She did not record the early echo.

The iron door scraped open behind her.

“You missed the beginning,” said Var.

Her younger brother stood in the doorway wearing a seal-hide coat too expensive for his trade permits. Snow jewelled his dark hair. He had brought two cups of hot berry wine, one in each hand, and the expression he used when he intended to make a demand seem like a gift.

“I heard it,” Eira said.

“That is not the same as listening.”

“It travelled through six ceilings and contaminated a controlled test.”

“Father will be moved that his greatest performance reached even you.” Var gave her a cup. “Come upstairs. He has tuned the east chamber to the pitch of a thawing river. People are weeping into strangers.”

“People enjoy permission.”

“You say that as if enjoyment were a technical defect.”

She smelled the wine. Juniper, black berry, something sweet and imported. Tavran orange peel, perhaps. Var dealt in objects whose origins became vague at the city gate.

“What do you want?” she asked.

“To see my sister.”

“You saw me yesterday.”

“Yesterday I wanted a permit.”

“And today?”

“Today I have the permit.” He smiled. “So my motives are pure.”

Eira drank. Warmth moved through her chest with alarming speed. “The glacier returned an impossible interval.”

Var’s smile changed, not vanishing but becoming attentive. For all his carelessness, he never laughed at her work.

“How impossible?”

“Two breaths.”

“I don’t know what that means.”

“It means the western echo arrived early. Or I counted badly. Or the concert—”

“You do not count badly.”

Everyone else said this as reassurance. From Var it sounded like accusation.

She turned back to the pillar. “Sound travels faster through water than fractured ice.”

“There is always water under the shelf.”

“Channels. Not open bodies.”

“How large a body?”

“If the interval is accurate? Larger than the western vaults. Perhaps much larger.”

Var looked down, as though the hidden water might be directly beneath his boots. “And if it is?”

“It is not.”

“Eira.”

“One reading is weather. Two are coincidence. Three are information.” It was Sura Neth’s first law, spoken to every listening child until it became more instinctive than prayer. “I have one and a spoiled second.”

A cheer rolled through the ceiling. Pell had opened another resonator.

Var crouched beside the tablet. “You did not mark the first.”

“I marked the trial.”

“You wrote that the second was spoiled.”

“The first may also have been spoiled.”

“By a concert that had not started?”

She disliked him most when he paid attention.

Var set his wine aside. “How bad?”

“There is no bad yet.”

“How bad if there is?”

Eira pictured the shelf as the city maps showed it: a white plain lying against the black coast. Beneath, her mind supplied layers. Gravel frozen into blue pressure ice. Old channels. Trapped air. Basalt teeth. She placed an unknown darkness west of the city and immediately erased it.

“If enough water gathered behind a sealed ridge,” she said, “it would push.”

“Toward us?”

“Water does not have a destination.”

“Everything has a destination.”

“That is a trader’s superstition.”

“Can you test again?”

“At dawn.”

“Why not now?”

She gestured upward as the ceiling shook.

Var took her clay tablet and pressed his thumb into an unused corner. “There. First witness.”

“That records nothing.”

“It records that you heard something before you persuaded yourself you did not.”

She reached for it. He held it away.

“I will break your finger.”

“No, you will describe why breaking it is statistically unsound.”

The lights shifted.

Not the small movement of oil flame. Every lamp in the vault leaned west at once.

Eira and Var went still.

A sensation passed through the floor—not a sound, not yet, but pressure. The berry wine quivered in its cups. Somewhere beyond the wall, a child laughed during Pell’s concert.

Then the deep note arrived.

It entered through the basalt pillar and spread into the floor. The pitch lay below hearing. Eira felt it in her teeth. The clay tablet crept a finger-width toward the edge of the table.

Var’s face emptied.

The note faded.

From overhead came an eruption of applause.

They thought it belonged to the music.

Eira took the tablet from her brother and marked the clay so hard the stylus snapped.

---

Pell found her after midnight in the passage outside the Hall of Names.

The performance had ended. The audience drifted past carrying boots and cups, flushed by warmth and communal sorrow. Some touched Pell’s shoulder as they went. A woman kissed both his cheeks and told him he had made her remember a brother dead thirty years. Pell accepted gratitude with lowered eyes and the humility of a man who had spent weeks imagining exactly this response.

His hair, once black like Var’s, had gone silver at the temples. He wore the sleeveless tunic of a stone singer, exposing forearms powdered with basalt dust.

“You came,” he said when he saw Eira.

“I was below.”

“That is not what I meant.”

“It reached the Third Vault.”

“Did it?” His face brightened. “Even the low opening?”

“All of it.”

He heard criticism where she had offered fact. Pell had always been greedy for meanings.

“You opened the north resonator after the foot sequence,” she said. “Then east. Then the river chamber.”

“You did hear.”

“The last note was not yours.”

Around them, people continued into the night. No one noticed Pell’s expression close.

“What note?”

“Deep pressure. Long duration. West to east.”

“You are asking whether I caused it.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“You used no buried instrument? No secondary weight?”

“I said no.”

His offence came quickly, too quickly. Eira looked at his hands. Stone dust had settled in every line. Her mother’s hands had looked the same after instrument days.

Pell lowered his voice. “Must you turn everything I make into one of her tests?”

The passing crowd seemed suddenly loud.

“This is not about Mother.”

“Everything between us is about your mother.”

Eira held up the tablet. Var’s thumbprint dented one corner; her broken stylus had cut a dark trench across the other. “Then be useful to her memory. Tell me exactly what you opened and when.”

Pell glanced at the marks but did not take the tablet.

“Come tomorrow,” he said. “I will show you the sequence.”

“At dawn I strike the western marker again.”

“Then after dawn.”

He looked suddenly tired. Not old—Eira could not think of her father as old—but exhausted by a conversation begun eleven years earlier and never properly ended.

“Eira,” he said, “sometimes a city is allowed to hear a note without being warned by it.”

Before she could answer, he joined the last of the departing audience.

She stood alone under the arch.

Far below, beyond eight floors of basalt and gravel and dark, the ice kept whatever answer it contained.

At dawn, the western marker did not respond.

# Chapter Two
## A Shore That Moved
### Lake Aruun · 408 days before the Flood

Seli Nav knew the marker had not moved because stones disliked initiative.

People invented purposes, changed partners, crossed forbidden water, woke with revelations, lied about them and called the lying maturity. Stones remained where they had been placed.

This one stood knee-high in the shallows, a clay cone painted with three blue lines. Tem had set it at the end of the previous dry season to mark the eastern route into Wren Harbour. Seli remembered because he had let her press the northern face while the clay was wet. The print of her hand remained beneath the waterline.

It should have stood four boat-lengths inland.

She circled it in the narrow canoe.

Morning stretched silver across Lake Aruun. There was no opposite shore, only a seam where water entered sky. Reed islands floated to the south, dark green and gold, carrying the calls of cranes. Far behind Seli, the flotilla of Seven Boats woke in layers: cook smoke, shell chimes, babies, paddles knocking hulls, Iri-of-Seven-Boats shouting at someone to stop urinating into the washing channel.

Seli shipped her paddle and leaned over the cone.

The three painted lines distorted under clear water. Tiny fish worked at algae growing in the impression of her hand.

The marker had not moved.

The shore had.

She looked east. Mud flats extended pale and glistening beyond the reeds. Last season, water had reached the roots of the crooked willow. Now a strip of dark wetness climbed its trunk above her head.

High water and low water at once.

“Seli!”

Her father’s voice crossed the lake.

Tem Nav approached in the family boat, standing at the stern with one hand on the steering oar. He was broad through the shoulders, his hair bound with the red cord of a route-master. Two apprentices sat amidships trying to appear uninterested.

“You missed first meal,” he called.

“The marker is wrong.”

“Markers generally are. That is why they need navigators.”

Tem brought his boat alongside and looped a line around her bow. His smile was gentle, as if humour might coax her back to ordinary concerns.

“The water is above last season’s wet mark,” Seli said, “but the shore has withdrawn twelve lengths.”

Tem glanced toward the willow. “Wind.”

“There has been no east wind for six days.”

“Pressure from the northern bays.”

“Would raise this basin.”

“Not if the southern reeds are drinking.”

“Reeds do not drink twelve boat-lengths before breakfast.”

One apprentice laughed and immediately disguised it as a cough.

Tem’s smile remained, but his eyes sharpened. “Show me.”

They poled to the willow. Seli laid her palm against the dark band and described the pattern she had been building in her mind. The lake was not a surface but a folded sheet, rising along one crease and falling along another. Tem asked good questions. He always asked good questions when they were alone.

“Could the basin floor have shifted?” he said.

“Not without a tremor.”

“Did you feel one?”

“No.”

“Then begin with the likely answer.”

“The likely answer is that I made an error?”

“The likely answer is local wind.”

“There was no wind.”

“There is always wind somewhere on Aruun.”

Tem turned to the apprentices. “What Seli has found is called a divided set.”

She stared at him.

He continued in his teaching voice. “When evidence points in opposite directions, do not invent a wonder large enough to contain it. Enlarge your field of observation. Renn, take soundings north. Apo, inspect the lower paint on the cone. We will compare at evening water.”

The apprentices nodded. Tem had transformed her discovery into a lesson so smoothly that even Seli almost admired it.

“You knew none of this before I showed you,” she said.

Tem kept his gaze on the willow. “We are all one household on the water.”

“Then the household can say my name.”

The apprentices became profoundly interested in rope.

Tem sighed. “Seli found the divided set.”

“Not to me.”

“Seli.”

“To the harbour council.”

“If it survives a day of measurement.”

“It survived your disbelief.”

“That is not a unit.”

He smiled again, but the gentleness no longer reached her.

From the flotilla came a sequence of bells: three quick, one long. First meal ending. The chimes overlapped from boat to boat until the harbour became a shaken bracelet.

A quieter sound moved beneath them.

Seli looked down.

The water against the hull had changed direction.

Small ripples travelled north while the visible surface drifted south. Two currents, one over the other. For an instant they appeared in her mind as colours: the upper lake thin and yellow, the lower dark blue, sliding apart like hands releasing.

“Do you feel that?” she asked.

Tem knelt and put his fingers in the water.

Before he answered, a long scaled back broke the surface beside the willow.

The sturgeon was longer than Seli’s canoe. Its armour held the dawn in dull plates. It rolled once, exposing a mouth pale as mushroom flesh, then disappeared northward.

Another surfaced beyond it.

Then five.

Sturgeon did not enter the eastern shallows until spring spawning. Spring lay ninety-three days away.

The apprentices stopped pretending calm.

Tem watched the last ring close.

“Wind,” Seli said.

He gave her a warning look, but fear had entered it.

They returned to Seven Boats in silence.

---

Kiri Drenn sat on a roof mat untangling blue cord from her hair.

She was Maru’s mother, though no one looking at them together would have guessed it. Maru moved like a post driven into harbour mud; Kiri drifted. Age had drawn the certainty out of her body but left moments of impossible brightness in her eyes.

Seli climbed onto the roof to escape Tem’s discussion with Iri. Below, the flotilla mother was already converting the moved shore into questions of mooring rights.

Kiri held up the cord. “Is this mine?”

“It is in your hair.”

“That was not my question.”

Seli sat beside her. “It is Omi’s fog cord. You borrowed it yesterday.”

“Then it has learned nothing from the journey.” Kiri dropped it in Seli’s lap. “The lake stepped backward.”

Seli turned. “Who told you?”

“You did.”

“I just arrived.”

“Then you are late.”

Kiri resumed searching her hair for another cord. Seli knew better than to force sequence upon her. Memory came to Kiri as weather came to Aruun: locally, without respect for prediction.

“What happens after the lake steps backward?” Seli asked.

Kiri hummed.

The melody had only four notes. First rising, second repeated, third dropping below the first, fourth held until breath failed.

“That is a child’s rope song,” Seli said.

“No. Children stole it.”

“What are the words?”

Kiri’s fingers stopped.

“The lake steps backward,” she sang softly, “to gather its long skirts.”

Below them Iri shouted about anchors. Tem answered. A baby began to cry on the third boat and was answered by one on the fifth.

“And then?” Seli prompted.

Kiri looked toward the open water.

For a moment her face was wholly present.

“Then it runs.”

A shell chime rang though no wind touched it.

# Chapter Three
## Salt in the Orange Root
### Tavra · 391 days before the Flood

The first tree died from the bottom upward.

Nara Ved noticed because trees usually kept their deaths in the crown. Leaves paled. Small branches withdrew. Fruit sweetened desperately in a final attempt to be eaten and carried elsewhere. Only then did the roots surrender.

The bitter-orange tree in Terrace Nine remained green and fragrant while its roots turned black.

Nara lay on her stomach in warm mud, one arm buried to the shoulder beneath the stone lip. Water moved around her skin. Above, copper mirrors guided morning through the cavern in bright rectangular steps. Each terrace caught a piece of Tavran dawn: moss silver, barley green, orange leaves almost blue against black basalt.

She reached deeper.

The root came apart between her fingers.

“Rot?” asked Mele.

The girl crouched at the channel with a basket she claimed contained pruning tools. It clinked like stolen medicine jars whenever she moved.

“Roots rot from drowning,” Nara said.

“Everything here is wet.”

“Wet is not drowned.”

“Words gardeners use to charge more.”

Nara withdrew her arm. Mud glazed it to the shoulder. Mele had the narrow, underfed look of a lower-terrace child and the direct gaze of someone who had learned adults lied most often while being kind.

“You are not assigned to this cavern,” Nara said.

“I am not assigned anywhere.”

“What is in the basket?”

“Pruning tools.”

“Show me.”

“No.”

Nara liked her immediately, which was inconvenient.

She cut a finger-length of root and carried it to the mirror step. The inner flesh should have been ivory. It was grey and translucent.

Mele leaned close despite herself. “Can it spread?”

“Everything spreads.”

“Gardener words again.”

Nara touched the root to her tongue.

Mele made a sound of disgust.

Salt flowered across Nara’s mouth.

Not the mineral bite of Tavra’s springs. Sea salt: bitter, organic, carrying the faint taste of weeds and deep green shadow.

She spat into the channel.

The western sea lay beyond three ridges and far below the terrace mouths. Between coast and cavern stood enough basalt to build Tavra twice.

“What?” Mele asked.

Nara tasted again.

“Nothing you should copy.”

“You licked rot.”

“Yes.”

“Was it useful?”

“Yes.”

Mele considered this and stole the root fragment from her hand. Nara caught her wrist before it reached her mouth.

“Useful once,” Nara said.

Footsteps struck the upper stair.

Mele twisted free, seized her clinking basket and vanished behind a curtain of hanging moss. She knew the service cuts better than registered workers. Nara watched the leaves settle, then turned as Chief Gardener Ossa descended.

Ossa wore her grey hair in the twelve coils of office. Two water assessors followed with clay boards under their arms. She did not look at the tree first. She looked at Nara’s muddy shoulder.

“You opened the feeder without authorization.”

“I opened the root lip.”

“Terrace Nine is under yield measure.”

“The tree is dead.”

“It is green.”

“Its leaves have not learned yet.”

One assessor smiled and stopped when Ossa glanced at him.

Nara held out the root. “Taste.”

Ossa did not take it.

“What am I tasting for?”

“Sea.”

The cavern changed around the word. Water continued down its channels. Bronze beetles clicked beneath orange leaves. Far above, mirror attendants adjusted the light and sent a golden bar sliding across the wall.

Ossa took the fragment.

She smelled it but did not touch it to her tongue. “Which channel?”

“Ninth feeder.”

“Source?”

“Red Spring.”

“Red Spring is not saline.”

“This root is.”

“Fertilizer runoff.”

“Would kill leaves first.”

“Improper copper.”

“Would stain the flesh blue.”

Ossa’s expression hardened with each answer. “You have become certain quickly.”

“I am certain it should be measured.”

“And until it is?”

“We close the feeder. Inspect every root bed on the western terraces. Taste the springs.”

The younger assessor shifted. Closing western feeders would affect seventeen thousand fruit trees and half the medicinal moss in Tavra.

Ossa handed the root back. “Burn the tree.”

Nara thought she had misheard. “To stop what?”

“Contagion.”

“Salt is not contagious.”

“We do not know it is salt.”

“You would know if you tasted it.”

The assessors became interested in their boards.

Ossa stepped close enough that Nara smelled cedar oil on her collar. “A First Accord delegation arrives in nineteen days. Orun complains that Tavra has undercounted winter stores. Suun claims priority over glass shipments. Kheled threatens to reassess water exchange. If an unverified disease closes the western gardens now, every bargaining table begins with our weakness.”

“The tree does not know about the Accord.”

“No. That is why people govern trees.”

“People keep trees alive.”

“And cities.”

Ossa looked up into the green crown. Small white blossoms had opened overnight, filling the cavern with sweetness.

“Burn it before midday,” she said. “Examine the neighbouring beds quietly. You will report to me alone.”

She turned to the assessors. “Terrace Nine exceeds projected health. Record it.”

Nara stared at her.

The older woman met her gaze without apology. “A green tree,” she said. “As you observed.”

They climbed toward the light.

Mele emerged before their footsteps faded. “You are going to burn it?”

Nara sat beside the channel.

The child opened her basket. Inside lay six jars of medicinal moss, a copper knife and three barley cakes wrapped in a stolen civic notice.

“You are a poor thief,” Nara said.

“No one caught me.”

“I caught you.”

“You did nothing about it.”

“I am considering the paperwork.”

Mele sat too. Together they looked at the orange tree.

“Does it hurt?” the girl asked.

Nara wanted to say that trees did not hurt in a way either of them could understand. She wanted to explain signalling through root and leaf, the sealing of wounds, the slow redirection of sugar away from damaged limbs. Instead she imagined salt entering every fine root hair, water present everywhere and none of it drinkable.

“Yes,” she said.

Mele accepted this.

At midday, Nara cut the tree down.

Its blossoms filled the cavern with fragrance as it burned.

---

That evening the city ate at the copper tables.

Tavra’s tables ran the length of each neighbourhood court, polished metal surfaces reflecting lamps, faces and shared dishes until every meal appeared twice. Nara sat between Beren Sarr and an elderly mirror cleaner who believed orange peel cured joint pain if applied to the feet.

Beren set down a platter of black-barley cakes.

“You smell like a funeral for a forest,” he said.

“One tree.”

“One tree can be a forest if it is the tree you love.”

Beren was broad, flour-dusted and incapable of allowing silence to become defensive. His husband, Ilyon, had not yet arrived. Empty space had been left for him across from Nara, although communal tables officially recognized no reserved places.

“Where is the Voice?” someone called.

“Practising humility,” Beren said. “It requires privacy and several assistants.”

Laughter moved down the table.

As if summoned by it, Ilyon entered the court.

People made room without appearing to. Hands touched his sleeve. He remembered the names of three children and asked after a woman’s injured sister before reaching his seat. Ilyon Sarr had the rare gift of making attention feel like a resource he possessed in endless supply.

He kissed Beren’s floury temple.

“You started without me.”

“We considered starving in protest, but the children opposed it.”

Ilyon sat across from Nara. “Chief Gardener Ossa says western yield is excellent.”

The speed of the message chilled her more than its content.

“She said that today?”

“To the provision council. It was the only brief report anyone applauded.”

Beren placed food on his husband’s plate. “Do not discuss stores while chewing. It gives every meal the flavour of shortage.”

Ilyon obeyed for almost three breaths.

“Orun has lost contact with a western station,” he said quietly.

Nara looked up.

“Storm?”

“No storm. The delegation is still coming, but they are sending a listener with it.”

“What does a listener listen to?” Beren asked.

“The ice.”

Beren considered the ceiling. “Does it say anything useful?”

“Usually where not to build.”

Nara’s tongue remembered salt.

Around them, the neighbourhood passed bowls and complaints. A toddler slept beneath the table. Two apprentices flirted by insulting each other’s knife technique. Someone began a water-drum rhythm at the far end, soft enough not to interrupt conversation.

The old mirror cleaner beside Nara rubbed orange peel against her wrist.

“Give me that,” Nara said.

The woman surrendered it, puzzled.

Nara tasted the white pith.

Sweet oil. Bitter skin. No salt.

“Are the Oruni frightened?” she asked.

Ilyon glanced toward the listening children nearest them and chose his words.

“They are attentive.”

It was the sort of answer that had made him beloved.

Nara thought of Ossa recording a dead tree as healthy.

She put the peel down.

“Those are not the same thing,” she said.


# Chapter Four
## The Animals Face East
### The Wind Steppe · 366 days before the Flood

The mammoths turned east during the night.

Oru knew before he opened his eyes. The ground felt wrong beneath his cheek.

He slept on a hide mat inside a wagon whose wheels had not moved in three days, yet a soft vibration travelled through the axle. Not thunder. Too continuous. Not bison. Too measured. The herd was walking, each broad foot pressing cold soil, while the bells braided into their neck wool gave no sound.

Someone had removed the clappers.

Oru sat up.

His wife’s sleeping place was empty. So were his boots.

Outside, dawn had not reached the steppe. A blue darkness lay over grass silvered with frost. The horizon ran unbroken in all directions except north, where the ice made a pale bruise against the sky.

A hundred mammoths moved east in single file.

The matriarch, Crooked Tusk, led. Calves walked between their mothers. The young bulls that ordinarily had to be driven from winter camp followed without complaint. There was no panic, no trumpeting, none of the jostling that came before a storm. Their silence made the movement terrible.

Oru’s daughter stood beside the dead fire holding his boots.

“You hid the clappers,” he said.

Pala was thirteen, serious in the theatrical manner of adolescents who expected the world to ask for their judgment at any moment.

“They were waking everyone.”

“They were supposed to.”

“You said never interrupt Crooked Tusk when she is certain.”

“I said never stand in front of her.”

“It means the same thing if she is walking.”

Oru took his boots. “Where is your mother?”

“Following.”

“Of course.”

Two other wagons had woken. People emerged pulling coats over nightclothes. A boy ran after the herd until an aunt caught him by the collar. Dogs whined but would not leave the wheels.

Oru laced his boots and started east.

The herd followed an old river course, a shallow fold in the grassland where sage grew tall. Their pace was not fast, but it admitted no hesitation. His wife, Sena, waited half a morning ahead beside a mound of glacial stones.

“You took your time,” she said.

“You took my breakfast.”

She handed him a barley cake.

Sena was smaller than Oru and three times as difficult to surprise. Her fur hood framed a face lined by sun and wind. She had carried Crooked Tusk’s first calf through a spring blizzard twenty-seven years ago; the matriarch had permitted Sena near every newborn since.

“They crossed the north trail,” Sena said.

Oru stopped chewing.

The herd had followed the north trail since before his grandmother’s grandmother. It ran west toward calving valleys below Orun, where warm springs kept winter grass exposed. Even in drought years, even when hunters and cave lions crowded the route, the matriarchs went west.

Ahead, Crooked Tusk reached a fork invisible to anyone who did not know it. She did not pause. East.

“Could another herd have claimed the springs?” Oru asked.

“No scent.”

“Fire?”

“Wind is from there.” Sena lifted her chin to the west. “No smoke.”

Oru studied the ground. Mammoth tracks overlaid reindeer, hare and wolf. All pointed east.

Not migration, then.

Departure.

He put his palm against one of the glacial stones. It was a habit borrowed from Oruni traders, who believed every rock had something to report if touched solemnly enough.

The stone trembled.

Barely.

Oru removed his hand and touched it again. Nothing.

Sena watched him. “Did the rock share its wisdom?”

“It says rocks are cold.”

“A costly education.”

They walked until sunrise ignited the grass. Birds lifted ahead of the herd in dark folds. A saiga herd appeared on the southern rise, running east so fast their breath trailed behind them.

Sena stopped.

“We take the wagons,” she said.

“Where?”

“Where they go.”

“There is no spring marked east for six days.”

“The elephants carry water knowledge older than your marks.”

“Crooked Tusk also eats rope.”

“She has never led us into thirst.”

Oru knew the argument was already over. Among the Roadless, decisions belonged to whoever accepted the cost of being wrong. Sena had packed before waking him.

“We warn the western camps,” he said.

“Send a rider.”

“They will not move because a rider says animals feel uneasy.”

“Then they will remain.”

“And Orun?”

At the city’s name her mouth tightened.

Twenty winters earlier, Oru’s people had guided Oruni grain caravans through whiteout after a harvest failure. In thanks, the city opened its south pasture for three years. On the fourth, Oruni stone houses appeared beside the springs. On the fifth, guards called Oru’s family trespassers.

“Orun has listeners,” Sena said. “Let them listen.”

“To rock. Not mammoth.”

“That is their choice.”

A calf stumbled in the old riverbed. Crooked Tusk stopped until it regained its feet. The entire line stopped with her.

Oru thought of an immense intelligence distributed through mothers, daughters, remembered paths, smell and the pressure beneath foot. No council. No vote. No speech pretending doubt was dignity.

Pala came running behind them, furious at having been left.

“Wagons are turning,” she announced. “Aunt Vey says you are a fool.”

“She has always been perceptive.”

“She says we should go west.”

“Then she should.”

Pala looked at the animals. “Will Orun go east?”

Oru watched Crooked Tusk resume her impossible path.

“No,” he said. “Cities rarely move before the ground beneath them does.”

---

Korun Bale found him at midday.

The old Oruni hunter appeared alone from a strip of juniper, dragging a narrow sled over bare grass. He wore white hides streaked with mud and carried no visible weapon. A cave lion’s footprint had been painted in ochre on his throat.

Roadless children had been taught that Oruni took steppe children who wandered too near the ice. Oruni children, Oru suspected, heard similar things about them. In practice, both groups mostly took each other’s trade goods.

Korun raised one hand.

“You stole our mammoths,” Oru called.

“I returned them poorly.”

He approached the stones and lowered his sled. It carried casts of animal tracks made in wet clay, each protected by grass.

“Lions?” Oru asked.

“Seven. Two males, three females, two young. Moving east.”

“All at once?”

“All night.”

Korun looked beyond him at the mammoth line. His face did not change, but he touched the ochre mark at his throat.

“What are they leaving?” Oru asked.

“The question is where.”

“No. Where is what people ask when they intend to follow. What are they leaving?”

Korun met his eyes.

“The western shelf.”

A cold wind crossed the steppe from that direction.

“Your city knows?”

“My city knows a marker station has failed to answer.”

“That is not what I asked.”

Korun crouched by the glacial stone and laid his ear against it. This time the vibration came clearly enough that Oru saw a tiny flake of frost dance from its shadowed side.

The hunter stood.

“I will go back,” he said.

“Will they listen to you?”

“No.”

“Then why?”

Korun gathered the track casts. “A person does not warn others because warning will succeed.”

“That seems a poor use of a journey.”

“He warns them so their choice remains theirs.”

Oru disliked the answer because it followed him after the hunter left.

By evening, the Roadless wagons rolled east behind the mammoths.

Oru rode west.

# Chapter Five
## A Country Without a Name
### The Reed Margin · 342 days before the Flood

Kes woke because frogs had stopped.

They lived everywhere in the reed margin: green frogs on roof mats, brown frogs in water jars, tiny gold frogs sleeping inside trumpet flowers. At night they called until the village seemed held together by their voices.

Now there was only rain.

Kes lay beneath a woven blanket, listening. Water tapped the roof lightly, almost politely. Beside him, his cousin Seliya snored with her mouth open. Grandmother Isha slept behind the reed partition, or pretended to. She had not slept properly since Luma left for Suun.

He sat up.

The floor mat was wet.

Not damp from rain. Water moved through it in a thin, cold sheet.

Kes touched Seliya’s shoulder. “The house is leaking underneath.”

She swatted him without waking.

He crawled to the door.

Their house stood on a platform two arm-lengths above ordinary high water. Kes had helped replace the piles last spring. He knew the view from the threshold: family canoes tied beneath, Grandmother’s eel pots to the left, the ceremonial reed bed beyond, then channels winding silver toward the unseen lake.

The canoes floated level with the door.

One bumped gently against the frame.

Kes stared.

Rain fell straight down. There was no wind. The water had risen silently through the night, swallowing platforms, smoke huts and garden mounds. Across the channel, the roof of Uncle Matu’s house appeared as a black island. Someone stood on it holding a baby above their head.

“Grandmother.”

His voice came small.

Then the eastern storage tower collapsed.

The sound woke the village. Reeds snapped like fire. People shouted from rooftops. A goat swam past with its tether trailing.

Isha tore down the partition. She was already dressed.

“Wake Seliya. Take the map basket.”

“What is happening?”

“The water has arrived.”

“From where?”

“All water comes from somewhere. Move.”

Grandmother had a way of making unanswered questions feel childish. Kes kicked Seliya awake and dragged the map basket from its hook. It was heavier than he expected, packed with clay shoreline pieces, knotted route cords and rolls of painted bark.

Isha shoved the canoe nose through the door. Water entered with it.

A drum sounded from the meeting platform: fast, uneven impacts. Not an alarm pattern. Someone was beating because any noise was better than none.

“Where is the high mound?” Seliya asked.

“Under water,” Isha said.

“The shrine?”

“Under.”

“The crane hill?”

Isha looked north. Only reeds moved there, their tassels barely above the surface.

“Boat,” she said.

They climbed in.

All around them the village loosened from the world. Roof mats lifted. Clay ovens dissolved into brown clouds. The poles where every family had cut arrival marks disappeared one by one.

Kes held the map basket against his chest.

He had never liked the clay pieces. They showed each island and channel from above, as if viewed by a bird. People did not live from above. From inside the reeds, a route was the smell of mint at one turning, the scratch of a leaning willow at the next, the place where insects went suddenly quiet above cold springs.

Now all the smells were water.

Isha paddled toward the central nursery. Children crowded its roof while two adults tied doors and tables into a raft. Kes recognized Piri’s younger sisters, both naked, one still clutching a breakfast spoon.

“Take them,” Isha told him.

“Our boat is full.”

“Then make it more full.”

They pulled three children aboard. Then a fourth. The canoe settled until water lapped one finger below the rim.

“Enough,” said Seliya.

A boy clung to a chimney post ahead, crying without sound.

Isha turned toward him.

“Grandmother,” Seliya said.

“Move your feet.”

“There is no room.”

“Make grief smaller, then.”

They reached the boy. Kes knew him only as Two-Reeds because he was always drumming sticks against his legs. He had neither now. His fingers were blue.

The canoe sank another finger when he entered.

A current took them.

It did not belong in the village channels. Water ordinarily moved south toward Lake Aruun with lazy certainty. This flow came west, pushing across reed beds and through houses. Isha dug her paddle in. The canoe turned sideways.

“Other side!” she shouted.

Kes and Seliya paddled with their hands. The map basket slid. He caught it with one elbow.

Ahead, two channels met where the meeting platform had stood. Debris spun between them: mats, branches, a copper pot, a carved household bird. Beyond, larger boats moved on the lake channel.

Suun boats.

Kes saw blue sails through the rain.

“Here!” he screamed.

Others shouted too. The entire village called from rafts and roofs. One Suun boat changed direction.

Then a horn sounded from farther out.

The boat stopped.

Even at a distance Kes recognized the eastern harbour mark on its sail: three bars beneath a blue circle. Maru Drenn’s boats patrolled the trade approach.

“Here!” Kes shouted again.

The patrol turned broadside. A person lifted a speaking cone.

“Water sickness in the margin,” the amplified voice called. “Eastern harbour is closed. Proceed to north quarantine.”

Isha stopped paddling.

“What sickness?” someone screamed from a roof.

“Proceed north. Do not approach marked vessels.”

“There is no north channel!”

The patrol had already begun to turn away.

Kes stood. The canoe rocked dangerously.

“We are here!” he shouted. “We are right here!”

The blue sail diminished into rain.

Grandmother pulled him down.

“Remember the mark,” she said.

“I know the mark.”

“Remember who turned it.”

Her face frightened him. Not angry. Settled.

The western current strengthened.

Their canoe struck something beneath the surface. A roof ridge. The hull lifted, twisted and spilled them all into cold brown water.

Kes kept hold of the basket.

For a while there were only pieces: a child’s ankle, bubbles, Isha’s white braid, the canoe bottom above him. He kicked toward brightness and struck the hull with his head.

Hands seized his coat and pulled.

He came up coughing beside Seliya. The children clung to the overturned canoe. Grandmother was farther away, one arm around Two-Reeds, the other holding a floating roof beam.

“Take him!” she called.

Seliya pushed off, but the current swept the beam toward a stand of submerged willow.

Isha looked at Kes.

“Keep the country,” she said.

Then the willows closed around her.

He did not see her again.

---

They reached Suun three days later on a cargo raft that had ignored the harbour closure.

Forty-one people from the reed margin arrived with them. Kes knew because a clerk counted twice and complained that children kept changing places.

Maru Drenn waited at the landing in a rain cloak, surrounded by guards. He was a large man with a neat grey beard and the expression of someone inspecting damage to property.

“Which settlement?” he asked.

“South Reed,” Seliya said.

The clerk checked his board. “No registered settlement under that name.”

Kes stepped forward.

“It is on the maps.”

“What maps?”

He opened Isha’s basket. Half the clay pieces had dissolved. The rest lay broken among tangled cords.

Maru looked inside.

“These are family route marks,” he said. “Not civic maps.”

“They show the village.”

“The margin shifts every season.”

“Our village did not.”

Maru’s eyes moved to Kes’s face. For an instant, something human interrupted the administrator.

“What happened to your parents?” he asked.

“My mother is in Tavra.”

“Father?”

“Dead.”

“Guardian?”

Kes said nothing.

Seliya took his hand. She was only seventeen, but no one else remained.

The clerk pressed a blank tablet. “Temporary dependents. Origin: unoccupied southern wetland.”

Kes lunged.

He knocked the tablet from the clerk’s hand and stamped on it before anyone understood. Wet clay spread beneath his heel.

“It was not empty!”

A guard grabbed him. Kes kicked until his sandal flew into the water.

Maru raised one hand. The guard released him.

The harbour master picked up the ruined tablet.

“What would you have us write?” he asked.

Kes was crying now, which made him furious.

“Write the name.”

“South Reed?”

“No. That is what traders call it.”

The true name was long. It described frogs singing beneath yellow flowers where two channels joined. Kes spoke every part.

The clerk stared. “There is no space.”

“Use another tablet.”

Around them, forty-one people waited in rain.

Maru gave the tablet back to the clerk. “Record South Reed.”

Kes hated him then with a purity he would later miss.

He gathered the broken map pieces and carried his country into the harbour.

# Chapter Six
## The First Table
### Tavra · 319 days before the Flood

The First Accord had been designed for twelve voices and now contained eighty-three.

They filled the copper hall in ascending layers. City speakers occupied the low circle. Behind them sat water assessors, trade counters, seed keepers, route masters, witnesses, servants who pretended not to be advisors and advisors who pretended not to be frightened. The open roof framed a hard blue square of Tavran sky.

At the centre stood an empty table.

The first Accord had been negotiated there one hundred and seventy years earlier after Orun withheld winter glass, Suun closed its salt routes and Kheled redirected a southern river. Tradition required each delegation to place a bowl of water on the copper surface. At the end, the waters were poured together and used to feed a tree in the public court.

This year Orun brought ice.

Eira set the blue-white block in a basalt bowl. It began melting immediately.

Suun’s bowl came from Lake Aruun, cloudy with suspended clay. Kheled offered half a cup from a deep cistern, an amount that drew murmurs in drought. Ilyon poured Tavran spring water last.

Nara watched from the garden witnesses’ tier. She had not been invited by Ossa. Ilyon had asked for her after privately tasting the orange root.

Across the hall, Eira looked smaller than her warnings.

She wore plain Oruni wool and no speaker’s copper. Her dark hair had been cut unevenly at the jaw, perhaps with a work knife. Beside her sat First Speaker Rusk Vey, whose composure seemed less an expression than a climate.

Aven Tal of Kheled opened proceedings.

She stood between the bowls wearing pale linen marked with ochre bands of mediation. Nara had expected an elder. Aven was perhaps forty, with a narrow face and eyes that seemed to grant every object equal consideration.

“We are not gathered to determine whether fear is justified,” Aven said. “Fear is weather within the body. It requires no permission. We are gathered to determine what obligations evidence creates between us.”

Ilyon smiled faintly. He appreciated good sentences even when they endangered him.

Rusk spoke first.

“Two hundred and six days ago, Orun’s western marker returned an anomalous echo. Subsequent strikes failed when the marker station ceased responding. A surface expedition found no route through new fracturing.”

“New fracture is common at the season turn,” said Ossa.

“Not in that direction,” Eira said.

Her voice barely carried. Aven gestured for her to stand.

Eira did, reluctantly.

“The western shelf drains north through three basal channels,” she said. “At least, it did. Pressure now travels east. Either a new cavity has opened beneath the shelf or a large body of water has reversed the channels.”

“How large?” asked a Suun route master.

“Unknown.”

“How soon does it affect inhabited ground?”

“Unknown.”

“What is the likelihood of collapse?”

“Unknown.”

Unease became irritation around the hall. People could forgive danger more easily than uncertainty.

Simo Varr rose behind Ilyon. He was young enough to carry righteousness like a polished blade.

“Then what exactly does Orun ask us to do?”

“Measure,” Eira said.

Several delegates laughed.

She waited for them to stop.

“Your springs, lake levels, deep wells and tides. At matched intervals. We need to learn whether these changes are local.”

Seli Nav, seated among the Suun observers, leaned forward. Tem put a restraining hand near her wrist without touching it.

Ilyon saw.

“Navigator Nav,” he said. “You have something?”

Tem stood.

“My household has recorded divided water along the eastern basin.”

Seli’s face closed.

“Divided how?” Aven asked.

“Shore withdrawal despite elevated marks. Opposing currents at depth. Premature sturgeon movement.”

“Who first observed this?”

Aven’s question appeared casual. Tem hesitated exactly long enough.

“My daughter.”

Seli stood beside him but looked at Eira.

“In the eastern shallows,” she said. “The lake is folding.”

People smiled at the childish phrase. Eira did not.

“Folding where?” she asked.

Seli came down into the circle without permission. She took two cloths from the record table and laid one over the other, then pushed opposite corners. Ridges formed between her hands.

“The upper current goes south. Lower goes north. Water marks rise here while shore withdraws here.” She touched separate folds. “Not tide. Not wind.”

“Pressure displacement,” Eira said.

“Or seasonal basin change,” Tem added.

The connection between the two women had existed for less than a minute and already others worked to weaken it.

Nara stood.

Ilyon had told her to wait until called. She ignored him.

“Sea salt is entering Tavra’s western roots.”

Ossa turned slowly.

Nara placed the blackened root on the copper table.

“Red Spring feeds Terrace Nine through volcanic stone,” she continued. “The sea lies below and west. Salt appeared in living tissue before the spring itself tested saline.”

“You were instructed to report further findings,” Ossa said.

“I am doing so.”

“To my office.”

“The tree was alive in your office report.”

A silence opened.

Aven looked from Ossa to Ilyon. “Was a false yield submitted to the Accord?”

“An unverified plant illness was contained,” Ossa said. “Tavra’s water remains clean.”

“Today,” Nara said.

Rusk put both hands on the table. “Western pressure beneath Orun. Reversed deep currents in Aruun. Salt intrusion in Tavra.”

“And drought in Kheled,” said Aven. “Which may be ordinary.”

“Four events,” Eira said.

“Three and a drought,” Ossa replied.

“Five,” called a child’s voice from the doorway.

Everyone turned.

Kes stood between two hall attendants, wet-haired and barefoot on one side. He held a basket of broken clay.

Behind him, Luma Reed wore the formal indigo of a marsh ambassador. Her face had gone pale.

“Kes,” she whispered.

He looked at her as if at a person in a story who had been described incorrectly.

“The south margin drowned,” he said to the hall. “No storm. No river. Water came through the houses.”

Luma crossed the floor, but he stepped away from her.

“How many?” Aven asked.

“Forty-one reached Suun.”

“How many lived there?”

Kes gripped the basket.

“Two hundred and sixteen at last eel count.”

A record keeper whispered to another. “There is no registered—”

“It has a name,” Kes said.

He spoke it. The long marsh name entered the copper hall: frogs beneath yellow flowers, two joined channels, a place carried in one child’s breath.

No one knew where to put it in the record.

The ice in Orun’s bowl cracked.

The sound was small. Every person heard it.

---

They argued until night.

Measure, evacuate, investigate, conceal. Each verb arrived carrying a different number of possible dead.

Rusk requested joint listening stations. Simo demanded immediate public warning. Ossa warned that interrupted planting would create the famine they feared. Maru Drenn, speaking for Suun harbour capacity, described the reed-margin loss as “significant local inundation,” and Kes had to be removed after throwing a clay map piece at him.

Aven asked questions that made every certainty look thin.

Ilyon listened.

That was his celebrated gift. He listened until speakers discovered the generous version of themselves, then addressed that person. But as the hours passed, Nara saw his fingers worrying the edge of his copper band.

At dusk he called a recess.

They stood together beneath the open roof while servants brought lamps. No one mentioned that Orun’s ice had melted into the other bowls.

“You wanted evidence,” Nara said.

“I wanted enough evidence.”

“Enough for what?”

“To move eighty thousand people without killing them in the movement.”

“You cannot wait until a warning contains no danger.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

His charm left him. Beneath it was a boy in a crowd.

“When I was nine,” Ilyon said, “the mountain smoked for three days. A priest announced Tavra would be buried before dawn. No roads were assigned. No stores opened. Everyone ran uphill at once. My mother’s hand slipped from mine.”

Nara said nothing.

“Four hundred and twelve people died,” he continued. “The mountain did not erupt. I was found under three bodies.”

Across the hall, Eira and Seli bent over a cloth map. Kes sat apart from Luma, arranging broken pieces. Aven stood alone at the table, watching the mixed water reflect the first stars.

Ilyon put his copper band back into place.

“I do not fear warning people,” he said. “I fear what warning does after it leaves the mouth.”

Nara thought of salt travelling unseen through stone.

“And I fear what silence does,” she said.

When the Accord reconvened, they voted for measurement.

Not evacuation. Not public warning.

For sixty days, each culture would watch its own water and send records to Kheled. Orun would repair the western marker. Suun would sound the eastern basin. Tavra would test springs. Kheled would compare the moon and tides.

Aven pronounced the decision responsible.

Rusk called it a beginning.

Ilyon called it unanimous.

Eira called it late.


# Chapter Seven
## The Demonstration
### Orun · 287 days before the Flood

By the time the delegates reached Orun, someone had painted **THE ICE REMEMBERS YOUR LIES** across the south gate.

The words were in Tavran trade script. Rusk’s attendants scrubbed until the copper pigment became a pink cloud, more conspicuous than the accusation.

Eira watched from the upper bridge.

Below, the visiting caravan entered in fragments: Kheledi riders blinded by snow glare, Tavran gardeners appalled by the cold, Suun travellers stepping from sleds as though the ground’s refusal to move were a personal insult. Children leaned from windows to inspect them.

Seli saw Eira and raised both hands.

Eira returned one. Friend was too large a word for someone met twice, but she had thought in Seli’s folds for thirty-two days. Records had passed between them by courier—lake intervals, root salinity, pressure timings—and developed a shared grammar more intimate than conversation.

The joint demonstration would test that grammar before the four powers.

Pell found Eira on the bridge.

“You have become important,” he said.

“It is temporary.”

“Most importance is.”

He wore a stone singer’s coat but no performance dust. Since the Accord, he had given Eira access to every concert sequence and resonator. They had spoken often and improved nothing.

“The Hall is clear?” she asked.

“For three days. No drums, no feet, no civic choirs, no children testing echoes.”

“North chamber?”

“Sealed.”

“East?”

“Sealed.”

“River?”

Pell’s patience showed a hairline crack. “Sealed.”

“Show me.”

“You have inspected twice.”

“I am inspecting a third time.”

“One reading is weather,” he recited. “Two coincidence. Three information.”

“Mockery does not improve the law.”

“Perhaps not. It improves the morning.”

They descended together.

The demonstration used the First Vault, large enough for a hundred witnesses. Four listening pillars rose from the floor. Eira had chosen the western one, newly calibrated against a replacement marker installed closer to Orun. The old station remained unreachable beyond the fractures.

Sura Neth supervised assistants setting water clocks and recording boards. At sixty-two, the Mother of the Listening House possessed the compact force of a door barred from within.

“You are late,” she told Eira.

“By how much?”

Sura almost smiled. “Not enough to defend.”

Seli entered carrying a fired-clay disc. Nema Vey of Kheled followed, pale from the journey and irritated by everything including air temperature. Ilyon, Aven, Rusk and the other speakers took places on the witness benches.

Var slipped in last.

He wore his best seal-hide coat and a trader’s innocent face. Eira had not invited him.

“What are you doing?” she whispered.

“Observing history.”

“From outside.”

“The corridor has poor history.”

Rusk called the room to order.

Eira stood beside the striker cord. She had explained the procedure so often that words seemed detached from meaning.

“The western marker will release a stone weight after receiving our lamp signal. Its first impact travels directly here. Reflections follow through different layers. Stable intervals indicate stable structures. Altered intervals indicate physical change.”

“What interval do you predict?” Aven asked.

“Direct arrival at seven breaths. Known returns at eleven, sixteen and twenty-three. The anomalous return occurs at nine.”

“Would its absence disprove a western cavity?”

“No. A cavity may shift, drain or sit beyond the new marker.”

Simo Varr laughed from the benches. “Then your claim survives any result.”

“My uncertainty survives any result. The claim changes with evidence.”

“That is convenient.”

“No. Convenience is what you call an answer that remains still while the world changes.”

A few Oruni listeners tapped fingers against knees: restrained approval.

Eira pulled the cord.

Tower lamps opened along the ridge.

The room settled.

She pressed her palm to the pillar. Around her, trained listeners did the same. Seli put fingertips against stone and closed her eyes, trying to read solid ground as she read a hull.

The strike came at seven.

Known return at eleven.

Sixteen.

Twenty-three.

No nine.

Eira waited.

Only the slow texture of deep ice entered her hand.

Simo shifted triumphantly. Someone whispered.

“Again,” Eira said.

The marker keeper needed time to reset the weight. During the interval, Nema checked water clocks. Sura walked the pillar line, observing hand placement.

Second signal.

Seven.

A return at nine.

It came sharp and unmistakable.

Eira’s breath escaped. Seli opened her eyes.

Then another return struck at ten, louder than the direct note.

Eira jerked her hand away.

The pillar rang audibly.

People gasped. The deep tone rose into the vault and trembled in the copper lamps. It was too loud, too clean, full of a metallic edge no glacier made.

Sura shouted, “Stop the marker!”

Eira was already running to the north chamber.

She heard Pell behind her. The sealed door stood closed. She broke the wax on the latch and pulled it open.

Inside, a copper tuning plate hung against the wall.

Its surface still vibrated.

For a moment no one spoke.

Pell went past Eira and seized the plate, burning his palm on oscillating metal. “This is not mine.”

Sura entered. Her gaze moved from the plate to a fine copper line running through the wall.

“Attached to the pillar,” she said.

A hidden resonator.

The false return had been manufactured inside the city.

Eira turned to the doorway. Witnesses crowded there. Simo’s expression had become horror disguised as vindication. Ilyon looked at Rusk. Rusk looked at no one.

Var had disappeared.

---

They found the line’s release lever in a maintenance passage.

No one found who had pulled it.

By midday there were six explanations. Varo’s followers blamed Eira for arranging a miracle. Simo accused Orun’s council of creating fear to obtain Accord stores. Oruni gate houses searched Tavran luggage. Tavran delegates threatened to leave. Suun accused both sides of endangering shared measurement.

The genuine nine-breath return vanished beneath the false one.

That, Eira understood, had been the purpose.

Sura sealed the listening vaults. Rusk suspended all public warnings. Aven requested a formal inquiry.

Eira found Var near the south animal pens, packing a sled.

He did not pretend surprise.

“You left quickly,” she said.

“People had begun searching coats.”

“Should they search yours?”

“No.”

She waited.

Var tightened a strap. “Do you want me to say it differently?”

“I want you to tell me who attached the line.”

“I sell access. I do not ask how buyers decorate it.”

The cold seemed to narrow around them.

“You sold access to the First Vault.”

“To a maintenance passage. Three months ago. Before anyone planned a demonstration.”

“Who?”

“A buyer’s silence is the only product a trader sells once.”

“This is not orange peel or untaxed fur.”

“No. Which is why naming them will get me killed.”

Eira took hold of his coat. “The false note makes every true reading worthless.”

“I know.”

“Then say the name.”

Var looked at her hand until she released him.

“I never met the buyer. A middle agent paid in Tavran copper. The work order carried a listening seal.”

“Whose?”

He said nothing.

“Mine?”

“Mother Sura’s.”

Eira stepped back.

“Sura would not—”

“Seals are copied. You know that better than most.”

She did. Eleven years ago Var had copied Eira’s apprentice seal to obtain route authorization for a western expedition. The expedition on which their mother died.

The memory appeared without warning: Var at sixteen, white-faced beneath a borrowed hood; Eira screaming that the seal was not hers; their mother’s empty sled returning behind the rescue party.

Var looked away first.

“I am trying to learn more,” he said.

“You always say that after the damage.”

“And you always say nothing until certainty gives you permission.”

The cruelty landed because it was exact.

From the city came the low call announcing sealed gates. The delegates would remain until the inquiry ended.

Eira saw fear move through Var’s face. He masked it with annoyance.

“You cannot leave,” she said.

“Apparently not.”

“Good.”

“My affectionate sister.”

“I want you where I can find you.”

He met her eyes. “You did hear it, didn’t you? Before the false plate.”

“Yes.”

“Will anyone believe that?”

Eira looked toward the black geometry of Orun, built between white shelf and winter sea. High above, Varo’s followers had begun singing from the walls. Their hymn said ice was the first memory of the world and would keep Orun until the final star went dark.

“No,” she said.

For the first time in her life, evidence had arrived and made truth more difficult to tell.

# Chapter Eight
## Two Coats
### Orun · 281 days before the Flood

Tiri had stolen the mediator’s coat because it possessed unnecessary pockets.

People with one pocket kept something useful in it. A knife, tinder, dried fish. People with six pockets distributed their usefulness until none remained. They deserved the lesson.

She took it from the guest-house warming room while Aven Tal argued inside with First Speaker Rusk. Tiri had been carrying charcoal when she noticed the coat unattended. She put it beneath her work coat, which made three coats total and complicated walking.

No one stopped her.

Adults saw what clothing told them to see. An Oruni child in work grey belonged in corridors. A thin child belonged wherever food was missing. Tiri had built most of her life inside such conclusions.

She reached the old lamp stair and examined the prize.

Pocket one: writing stylus.

Pocket two: salt sweets.

Pocket three: spare glove ties.

Pocket four: a flat Kheledi prayer stone, warm from the coat.

Pocket five: nothing.

Pocket six had been sewn shut.

Tiri bit the thread and found folded bark inside the lining. Ochre marks covered it: Orun from above, the south road, the steppe and a series of red circles leading east.

Treasure.

Not coin treasure. Adults rarely drew maps to money; they remembered money more carefully than children. This was buried-object treasure. Perhaps ancestral lamps. Perhaps the glass bones Varo said had been placed beneath Orun before the ice.

At the bottom, someone had drawn little sleds beside numbers.

Tiri could count but not read trade script. She knew the Oruni sign for **stores** and the gate sign for **restricted**. Both appeared often.

Footsteps sounded above.

She folded the bark and pushed it inside her second coat, into the pocket where she kept things too valuable to admit owning.

A woman descended the stair carrying a lamp tray.

Iso Wren stopped when he saw her. Everyone called Iso a woman because his face remained soft and he wore his dead wife’s bead cord; Iso corrected no one unless ritual required it.

“That coat has become very large,” he said.

“I am growing.”

“Sideways.”

“It is winter.”

“It has been winter since you were born. Return it.”

Tiri clutched the lapels. “To whom?”

“The person whose shoulders fit it.”

“That could be anyone.”

Iso sat on the stair. He lit ancestral lamps all day and therefore never hurried. “Aven Tal. Guest chamber four. You may leave it at the door and preserve your criminal mystery.”

Tiri took a salt sweet from pocket two and put it in her mouth.

“Is there another?” Iso asked.

She gave him one.

They sat chewing.

From behind the lamp stair wall came the muffled argument.

“—not a public evacuation order,” Aven was saying. “A route assessment.”

“You mapped capacity,” Rusk replied. “You numbered sleds.”

“I would be negligent not to.”

“You would be inflammatory if those maps circulated.”

Tiri touched the bark through her coat.

“If the warning is false,” Aven said, “a plan causes no flood. If it is true, the absence of a plan causes deaths.”

“The last false evacuation killed hundreds.”

“In Tavra.”

“Panic recognizes no border.”

“You are very calm for a man whose western station has vanished.”

Silence.

Iso looked at Tiri. Tiri looked at the lamp tray.

“You heard nothing,” he said.

“I hear many things.”

“That is what worries me.”

The upper door opened. Footsteps approached.

Tiri ran down.

She knew the old stair ended at a sealed name passage, but halfway lay a ventilation slit narrow enough for a child. She pushed through and dropped into darkness.

The coat snagged. She twisted, heard cloth tear and fell onto cold stone.

Something moved nearby.

Tiri froze.

A second child whispered, “Do you have food?”

“Who are you?”

“Do you?”

Tiri struck flint. A spark revealed a boy her age crouched beneath the vent, barefoot and furious. His hair had been cut with no concern for symmetry. He held a basket of broken clay.

“I asked first,” Tiri said.

“No, I did.”

“You asked about food. That is not your name.”

“Kes.”

“Tiri.”

“Do you have food, Tiri?”

She gave him the remaining sweet from the mediator’s pocket.

Kes ate it before suspicion could object.

He had arrived with the Accord delegates and escaped the guest house after his mother tried to make him bathe. They sat beneath the vent comparing grievances. Kes’s country had drowned. Tiri did not say she was sorry. People said sorry when they wanted grief to finish quickly.

Instead she showed him the map.

Kes spread it across the stone.

“This is not treasure,” he said.

“You cannot read Oruni.”

“I can read numbers.” He touched the marks. “Sled capacity. Food loads. Days.”

“What does the red mean?”

“Road stages.”

Tiri considered. “A secret journey.”

“Evacuation.”

She disliked the word. It made every wall temporary.

Kes studied the eastward route. “Your people are planning to leave.”

“No.”

“Then why draw this?”

“Adults draw many things they are too frightened to do.”

He traced the south road until the bark ended. “Does it go to Tavra?”

“Eventually.”

“My mother is going back there.”

“You don’t like her.”

Kes folded the map. “That is not the same as wanting her drowned.”

The sentence stayed with Tiri.

They left the passage after dark. She returned Aven’s coat outside chamber four, lighter by one map and three sweets. She kept the spare glove ties because everyone lost ties.

The evacuation bark went into the secret pocket of her second coat.

Tiri did not know it would remain there until the day she had to choose between the map and a freezing child.

For now, she had acquired a country and a friend, neither of which belonged to her.

# Chapter Nine
## The Station Falls
### Western Shelf · 260 days before the Flood

The missing station stood beneath them.

Tovan Ash-Hand lay flat at the edge of the crevasse and looked down through fifty arm-lengths of blue ice. The building had fallen whole: four black walls, white roof, signal mast bent but attached. A lamp glowed behind one window.

“Tell me that is reflected sun,” said Nemi Blue-Bead.

“The sun is behind us.”

“Tell me it is a luminous worm.”

“It is a lamp.”

“Poor choice.”

Wind crossed the crevasse and made a bottle note. Nemi backed away on elbows, careful not to stand until she reached the rope line.

Tovan remained.

The station rested on a shelf inside the fracture, tilted toward darkness. The crevasse continued below it, narrowing into blue beyond sight. No rubble surrounded the building. The ice had opened, accepted it and partly closed overhead.

A person might still be alive inside.

The sensible thought was that lamp oil could burn for months in a sealed room.

The thought Tovan chose was rescue.

He touched the blackened skin across his right palm. The scar had given him his public name and taken most feeling from two fingers. Peleth used to warm that hand between his own after winter routes.

Peleth had been dead three years.

Tovan still arrived at facts late when he disliked them.

“We descend,” he said.

Hadran Vo swore behind him.

The master sledwright had come to inspect the replacement marker, not conduct a recovery. His beard collected frost as he argued.

“Crevasse wall is fresh. No anchors. No read on the lower shelf. We mark the location and return with a vault team.”

“Two days out, two back, three to assemble. Seven days.”

“The station has been missing for one hundred and forty.”

“Then seven matter greatly to anyone alive.”

“No one is alive.”

Tovan looked down at the lamp.

Hadran’s voice softened. “Captain.”

“Not captain.”

“You were demoted because this is exactly what you do.”

“Save people?”

“Turn your need to save people into their need to follow you.”

Nemi studied a rope buckle with devotional interest.

The accusation was one Peleth had made on their last expedition. Not in those words; Peleth had been kinder, which made remembering worse.

Tovan stood.

“We have two hundred lengths of line, four ice screws and a broad-runner sled.”

“Three screws,” Nemi said.

Hadran turned. “We packed four.”

“One fractured this morning.”

“You did not report it?”

“I am reporting it now.”

Hadran closed his eyes.

Tovan almost laughed. The sound rose from the same place as fear.

“Three screws,” he said. “Enough.”

“For a corpse recovery?”

“For a look through the window.”

“If the wall moves?”

“Pull me up.”

“If the wall moves, it will pull us down.”

“Then let go.”

Hadran stared at him. “You make death sound generous.”

“No. I make rope sound detachable.”

In the end, Hadran helped rig the descent because refusing would leave Tovan to rig it badly.

They drove screws beyond the fracture line and spread the load through Nemi’s low sled. She objected to using an unfinished design until Tovan pointed out surviving was an effective test. Hadran remained above with the brake. Nemi descended second to manage the approach.

The ice swallowed colour.

At ten lengths, Orun’s black coast disappeared behind the blue lip. At twenty, wind weakened. At thirty, Tovan heard water.

Not dripping. Flowing.

A river moved somewhere inside the glacier.

Nemi heard it too. Her face had gone solemn.

“That should be frozen,” she whispered.

“Tell it.”

At forty lengths, they reached the station roof.

Tovan cleared frost from the window. Inside, the lamp burned on a wall hook.

A woman sat beneath it.

Her back rested against the far wall. Her face had dried in cold air, skin drawn gently over bone. A clay tablet lay in her lap.

“No,” Nemi breathed.

Tovan tried the door. Ice sealed the lower half. Together they cut until it opened inward.

The room smelled of old oil, wool and the sweet mineral scent of trapped cold. Three other station keepers lay on sleeping platforms. They had arranged themselves before death, boots placed neatly below.

Tovan crossed to the woman.

He recognized her as Edda Mor, western keeper, although age and freezing had altered her. The tablet in her lap was covered edge to edge with pressure marks. Beside her stood a stack of twelve more.

Nemi lifted the lamp.

“It cannot have burned this long.”

Tovan inspected the reservoir. Nearly full.

“Someone filled it.”

“Who?”

The water sound deepened.

They looked at the floor.

A line of new frost ran between the stones. It widened as they watched.

“Nemi.”

“I see it.”

The station shifted.

Not falling. Rotating slowly as the ice shelf beneath gave way.

The rope tightened across Tovan’s harness.

Nemi seized the tablet stack. “Go.”

He looked at the bodies.

There was time for one, perhaps.

Peleth returned in memory with merciless clarity: beard frozen white, one leg trapped beneath the overturned sled, telling Tovan to stop digging because the storm had already taken the choice from them.

Tovan had kept digging until three other people almost died beside him.

He took Edda’s final tablet instead of her body.

They climbed onto the roof.

“Pull!” Tovan shouted.

The rope lifted. Nemi rose first, tablets strapped to her chest. The station rolled beneath Tovan. Its signal mast struck the wall and shattered.

Dark water erupted through the floor.

It was not melt dripping from above. It came under pressure from below, black and violent, filling the room around the seated dead.

The station dropped.

Tovan swung into the wall. His burned hand lost the rope. His other held. Above, Nemi screamed his name.

The building vanished into the blue throat, its lamp still visible through the window. For several breaths it sank like a star through deep water.

Then darkness took it.

Hadran and Nemi hauled Tovan over the crevasse lip near sunset.

No one spoke until they were three hundred paces away.

Hadran slapped him.

The old man’s arthritic hand made a weak sound. Tovan accepted it.

“You were right,” Hadran said. “Do you understand? That is not the same as being wise.”

Nemi knelt in the snow, checking the tablets. Two had cracked during the ascent. She placed their pieces together.

“Can you read this?” she asked.

The clay held rows of timing cuts. Tovan knew only route marks, but even he saw the pattern contracting from tablet to tablet.

They returned to Orun after four days.

Eira met them outside the Third Vault with Rusk, Sura, Aven and half the listening council. Rumour had travelled faster than sleds. Tovan gave Edda’s last tablet to Eira.

She read standing up.

No one interrupted.

At last Sura said, “Well?”

Eira’s thumb moved across the final marks.

“The western station detected a water body beneath the shelf.”

“How large?” Rusk asked.

“It exceeded their instruments.”

The corridor seemed to contract.

“And the early return?” Aven asked.

“Real.”

“Can the false demonstration be separated from these records?”

“Yes.”

“Will people believe that?”

Eira looked at Tovan. His face must have answered.

“What else?” Sura asked.

Eira turned the tablet over.

On the back, Edda had abandoned formal marks. She had written in ordinary Oruni.

Eira read aloud.

“**The water is not trapped beneath the ice. The ice is trapped above the water.**”

Far west, beyond wall and ridge and sight, a sound rolled beneath the world.

The lamps leaned in their brackets.

This time there was no concert.

Everyone in Orun heard it.
