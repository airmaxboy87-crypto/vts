# Before the Waters: A Global Compendium of Deluge Traditions

> **Research note for the Aethel world archive**  
> This is a comparative folklore catalogue, not evidence of one historical worldwide flood. “Myth” here means a culturally meaningful traditional narrative, not “a falsehood.” Many traditions remain sacred, some were recorded through colonial intermediaries, and several show signs of biblical or other post-contact influence. Names and spellings vary between translations.

No finite catalogue can contain *every* flood tradition. Hundreds of local versions exist, many are held within communities rather than published, and some have been lost. This survey prioritizes geographically broad coverage and distinguishes a world-ending deluge from coastal inundation, river flood, tsunami, cosmic winter and “earth-diver” renewal stories.

## How to read the catalogue

Each entry asks four questions useful for worldbuilding:

1. **What was the former world like?** Golden age, crowded cities, giants, failed humans, moral disorder or simply familiar country?
2. **Why did the waters come?** Divine judgment, human noise, broken taboo, wounded water-being, cosmic accident or natural overflow?
3. **What crossed the boundary?** A family, animals, seeds, fire, ritual knowledge, social law or nothing at all?
4. **What changed afterward?** Mortality, agriculture, languages, nations, coastlines, animal traits or a covenant?

## 1. Mesopotamia and the ancient Near East

### Sumer — Ziusudra and the Eridu tradition
The former world already possesses cities, kingship, farming, herds and temples: civilization is not invented after the flood but carried through it. The gods decide to destroy humanity; Enki covertly warns the pious priest-king Ziusudra through a wall. Ziusudra builds a huge vessel, preserves animals and “the seed of mankind,” survives seven days of storm, sacrifices, and receives eternal life in Dilmun.

### Old Babylon — Atrahasis
Humans are created as a labour force for the gods. Their multiplying population and noise disturb Enlil, who first tries plague and famine. Enki helps Atrahasis defeat both disasters, so Enlil sends a flood. Atrahasis seals his boat with bitumen and carries family and animals. After seven days, the horrified gods regret losing their worshippers. The post-flood settlement introduces mortality, infertility and social limits to population: the reset changes the biological rules of human life.

### Babylon and Assyria — Uta-napishtim in *Gilgamesh*
Shuruppak is an urban, wealthy world of artisans, silver, gold, kinship and domesticated and wild creatures. Ea tells Uta-napishtim to abandon wealth, demolish his house and build a six-deck, cube-like vessel carrying “the seed of all living things,” his household and craftspeople. The storm terrifies even the gods. After dove, swallow and raven tests, sacrifice restores relations between gods and humans; Uta-napishtim and his wife alone receive immortality.

### Berossus/Xisuthrus
In the later Greek-language account attributed to the Babylonian priest Berossus, Xisuthrus is ordered not only to build a vessel but to bury written records at Sippar. After the flood, survivors recover those records. This version makes archives—the memory of the first world—as important as biological survival.

### Ugarit
A fragmentary fourteenth-century BCE version from Ugarit preserves Atrahasis, the flood and the survivor’s immortality, showing the tradition beyond Mesopotamia proper.

### Hebrew — Noah
The antediluvian earth is violent and corrupt, yet populated by families, agriculture, metalwork, music, cities and long-lived ancestors. Noah preserves his household and animal kinds in a three-deck ark. Waters come from both the sky and “the deep.” Afterward come sacrifice, the rainbow covenant, permission to eat meat and renewed laws against murder. Later Jewish elaborations imagine pre-flood ease, enormous creatures, giants and a sapphire book of heavenly knowledge aboard the ark.

### Christian reception
Christian traditions inherit Genesis while making the ark an image of salvation and a renewed creation. Syriac and other apocryphal traditions sometimes place Adam’s bones and primal treasures aboard, so that the first human world is physically carried into the second.

### Islamic — Nūḥ
The Qur’anic account centres on prophecy, disbelief and moral choice. Nūḥ builds under divine guidance while mocked; believers, household members and pairs embark. One son trusts a mountain instead and drowns, stressing that kinship cannot replace righteousness. Waters rise from earth and sky; the vessel rests on al-Jūdī. Unlike many later retellings, the Qur’anic emphasis can be read as judgment on Nūḥ’s people rather than a detailed natural history of a globally submerged planet.

### Ancient Iranian rain purification
In a Persian cosmological tradition, Tishtrya/Tistar battles drought-demon Apaosha and releases immense rains that drown destructive creatures. Wind drives the water to the world’s edge to form the cosmic sea Vourukasha; poison washed from the land explains salt water.

### Zoroastrian — Yima’s vara
This is a close cousin, not a conventional rain-flood. Yima rules a deathless, disease-free and increasingly crowded golden world, enlarging the earth three times. Ahura Mazda warns of a killing winter followed by meltwater. Yima builds a sealed, radiant enclosure—the *vara*—for the finest humans, cattle, dogs, birds, plants, seeds, food and undying fire. It is a seed-vault and model biosphere preserving the best of creation through climate catastrophe.

## 2. Mediterranean and Europe

### Greece — Deucalion and Pyrrha
Zeus destroys a corrupt human age. Prometheus warns his son Deucalion, who escapes with Pyrrha in a chest or ark for nine days and lands on Parnassus (or another mountain in variant traditions). They restore humanity by throwing the “bones of their mother”—stones of Mother Earth—behind them. Humanity is therefore literally remade from landscape.

### Greek local deluges — Ogyges, Dardanus, Megarus and Samothrace
Greek tradition remembers more than one flood. Ogyges belongs to a remote first inundation; Dardanus leaves flooded Arcadia; Megarus follows crane cries to Mount Gerania; Samothracian tradition describes lowlands overwhelmed when a barrier between seas breaks, with drowned columns later caught in fishing nets.

### Roman — Ovid’s deluge
Jupiter chooses water rather than fire to end impious humanity, and Neptune releases rivers and seas. Deucalion and Pyrrha alone survive at Parnassus and repopulate the earth with stones. Ovid fills the catastrophe with displaced dolphins in forests, wolves among sheep and farmers rowing over drowned fields: ecological boundaries dissolve before creation is reordered.

### Philemon and Baucis
A regional moral flood rather than a universal one. Two disguised gods find every door closed except that of an elderly poor couple. The inhospitable valley is drowned while their cottage becomes a temple. The former world fails through refusal of hospitality.

### Norse — Ymir’s blood
Odin, Vili and Vé kill the primordial giant Ymir. His blood becomes a deluge that drowns almost all frost giants; Bergelmir and his wife escape in a *lúðr* (variously translated as a box, coffin, mill-container or boat-like object). The flood is part of cosmogony: Ymir’s flesh becomes earth, bones become mountains and blood becomes sea.

### Welsh — Dwyfan and Dwyfach
The lake of Llion bursts. Dwyfan and Dwyfach survive in a mastless vessel with pairs of living creatures, land in Britain and renew life. The form is known mainly from comparatively late Welsh antiquarian sources and should be used cautiously.

### Welsh — Cantre’r Gwaelod
A different kind of flood memory: a fertile lowland kingdom in Cardigan Bay is protected by sea defences and lost when the barriers fail through negligence or drunkenness. Bells of the drowned settlements are said to sound beneath the bay.

### Irish — Cessair and Fintan
In the medieval *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, Cessair reaches Ireland before the biblical flood. Most of her company die; Fintan survives through transformations into salmon, eagle and hawk, becoming a living memory of Ireland’s successive ages. This is Christian-era synthesis with native transformation lore.

### Lithuanian nutshell tradition
Pramžimas sends Water and Wind against a violent humanity. People and animals on the highest mountain survive inside a nutshell dropped by the deity. An elderly pair create new couples by leaping over “bones of the earth,” and nine tribes descend from them.

### Romani tradition recorded in Transylvania
Before the flood, humans live without death or labour; flesh grows on trees and rivers carry milk and wine. Broken trust around a mysterious fish brings the catastrophe. Survivors save kin, animals and seeds in a boat, but the renewed world now contains work, illness, slow reproduction and death—a particularly complete picture of a lost golden age.

### Baltic and Black Sea local legends
Traditions around submerged settlements, bursting lakes and the opening of the Bosporus often concern a particular coast rather than all humanity. They are valuable worldbuilding models because survivors retain precise landmarks, ruins and changed fisheries instead of describing a blank global reset.

## 3. South Asia, Himalaya and Central Asia

### Vedic India — Manu and the fish
Manu protects a tiny fish, moving it through progressively larger vessels as it grows. The fish warns him of a coming deluge. Manu builds a boat and ties it to the fish’s horn; it tows him to a northern mountain. He preserves himself rather than a zoo. Through sacrifice after the waters, a woman is born, and together they renew humanity. Later traditions identify the fish as Matsya, an avatar of Vishnu, and add seeds, sages and sacred knowledge.

### Bhil — Dhobi and the fish
A fish warns the devout washerman Dhobi. He and his sister survive in a gourd or boat while divine figures test the flooded world. The story combines local kinship, gourds, forest ecology and later Hindu motifs.

### Kamar of central India
A divine warning leads an old couple to hide inside a hollowed log with grain. A later test determines whether the earth has dried. Their descendants repopulate the world; in some tellings, incest is ritually disguised by changing terms of relationship.

### Assam and Naga-region variants
Numerous traditions preserve a brother and sister, couple or remnant on a high mountain or tree. The new human family often begins only after ritual signs approve their union. Floodwater may carve valleys out of a formerly level earth.

### Lepcha — Mount Tendong
The Lepcha remember waters rising until survivors reach Tendong Hill, which “raises its horn” or remains above the flood. The story is tied to a named sacred landscape in Sikkim and is commemorated in Tendong Lho Rum Faat observances.

### Tamil traditions of Kumari/Kumari Kandam
Tamil literary commentary remembers lands and learned assemblies lost to the sea. Modern “Kumari Kandam” developed by combining these traditions with nineteenth-century Lemuria theories, so ancient literary motifs, colonial science and modern identity should not be collapsed into one supposedly unchanged legend.

### Altaic — Nama
Ülgen orders the failing-eyed Nama to build an ark, which Nama’s sons construct on a mountain. Animals gather as water rises. Depth is measured with immense cables; raven, crow, rook and dove test the receding world. The failed birds remain with carcasses and receive their scavenging natures. The story explains constellations and animal behaviour as well as survival.

### Buryat and eastern Siberian ark traditions
A man secretly builds a ship at divine instruction. A devil discovers the plan through the man’s wife, enters the ark and becomes a mouse that gnaws its hull; a cat is created to stop it. A proud mammoth refuses refuge and dies. These forms may contain substantial Christian contact influence, but they creatively localize the story through Siberian animals and spirit relations.

### Tuvinian/Soyot — moving earth-supporter
A giant frog or turtle supporting the world shifts, letting the cosmic ocean rise. A foresighted elder and family survive on an iron-strengthened raft that comes to rest on a wooded mountain. Catastrophe comes from unstable cosmic anatomy, not human sin.

### Mongolian — Hailibu
The generous hunter Hailibu receives a dragon king’s stone that lets him understand animals, on condition that disclosure will petrify him. Hearing birds predict a mountain flood, he tells his people the secret to save them and turns to stone. The community survives because one person willingly becomes a permanent monument.

### Siberian peoples
Recorded Vogul/Mansi, Samoyed, Yeniseian, Kamchadal and neighbouring variants use poplar boats, willow-root cables, log rafts and mountain anchors. Floods disperse peoples and languages, eliminate animals later recreated by a god, or leave one young pair alive after hunger. Local technologies matter more than a standardized ark.

## 4. East and Southeast Asia

### China — Gun and Yu
This is not primarily a last-family-in-a-boat story. Under Yao, catastrophic waters persist for years. Gun tries to block them with self-expanding soil and fails; Yu studies terrain, dredges channels and leads water to the sea. The renewed world emerges through labour, measurement, hydraulic knowledge and political legitimacy. Civilization survives by learning to live with water rather than merely escaping it.

### China — Nüwa repairs heaven
After cosmic pillars break, sky and earth lose alignment; fire and flood ravage the world and predatory creatures attack people. Nüwa melts five-coloured stones to mend heaven, uses a great turtle’s legs as supports, kills a black dragon and checks the waters with reed ash. Restoration is architectural and cosmic.

### China — Fuxi/Nüwa sibling-survivor traditions
Among several Han and minority traditions, a brother and sister survive inside a gourd, drum or protected container and become ancestors after receiving supernatural permission to marry. These are distinct regional traditions, not one canonical Chinese flood story.

### Zhuang — Bubo and the Thunder God
A long southern Chinese cycle links conflict with the Thunder God, a released or stolen source of water, flood survival by siblings in a gourd, and the repopulation of the earth. The gourd functions as seed, womb and boat.

### Lisu, Lolo/Yi, Jino, Sui and other southwest Chinese traditions
Common motifs include a sibling pair in a wooden drum or gourd, animals that warn humans, thunder deities, tests sanctioning marriage and post-flood differentiation of peoples. Each belongs to a distinct living culture and should not be treated as interchangeable “Chinese Noah” material.

### Korea — Namu Doryeong, the Son of the Tree
A boy born from a laurel or divine tree survives a world-covering flood by riding the tree. He rescues ants, mosquitoes and another boy; grateful animals later help him complete impossible tests. He marries into the surviving household, and the two couples renew humankind. Compassion toward small creatures becomes the technology of survival.

### Ifugao — Kabigat and Wigan
A cosmic or divine flood fills valleys and reshapes the Cordillera. Siblings or paired survivors occupy opposite mountains, later discovering each other as waters fall. In related Ifugao cosmology, released waters carve mountains and valleys from a flatter former world.

### Philippine traditions
Atá survivors are carried home by a giant eagle; a Mandaya pregnant woman becomes the sole human remnant; Tinguian people seek mountain refuge. The diversity of Philippine narratives reflects distinct islands and peoples rather than a single national story.

### Andaman Islands — Puluga
People neglect commands given at creation, and creator Puluga sends a destructive flood. Survivors preserve fire—a central concern in an island forest world where fire is a social and sacred inheritance.

### Mainland Southeast Asia
Bahnar, Kammu, Karen, Chingpaw, Shan and other traditions use drums, gourds, rafts, mountaintops and sibling survivors. In many, animals or divine signs authorize the difficult founding marriage of the new world. Missionary-era recording makes influence difficult to untangle in some versions.

### Island Southeast Asia
Batak, Nias, Enggano, Dayak, Ot Danum, Toraja, Alfur, Rote and Flores/Nage traditions include earth-supporting serpents, drowned settlements, boats tied to mountain peaks, and small family remnants. Floods frequently explain island separation, rice origins, clan descent and why the dead or animals now behave as they do.

## 5. Africa

African deluge traditions are real but unevenly distributed, and “African Noah” lists often repeat colonial-era or demonstrably syncretic versions without caution.

### Yoruba — the anger of Olókun
In one creation-linked cycle, land is extended over the primordial waters without adequately honouring Olókun, sovereign of the deep. Olókun sends waters over the made land; ritual and mediation associated with Ọ̀rúnmìlà/Ifá restore balance. The story concerns jurisdiction, neglected relationship and the ocean’s prior claim—not simply wicked humanity.

### Mande/Bamana — Faro
Faro, an ordering water power associated with speech, fertility and social balance, participates in a complex cosmogonic cycle that includes destructive water and renewed order. Published summaries vary considerably; this tradition deserves treatment from Mande sources rather than reduction to an ark parallel.

### Maasai/Nandi — Tumbainot
A righteous Tumbainot receives warning after human violence and preserves family and animals in a wooden vessel. Dove and vulture test the waters; four rainbows announce the end. Its close Noachic structure and colonial recording history make contact influence a serious possibility.

### Cameroon — the goat’s warning
A girl allows a goat to eat flour instead of driving it away. In return, the goat warns her and her brother to flee an imminent village flood. After years alone, the goat returns and gives ritual means by which the siblings may become founders. Kindness to an animal carries the future.

### Kwaya — ocean in a pot
The ocean is once contained in a small vessel kept in a house. A forbidden touch breaks it, releasing the waters. The old world differs physically from ours: water is a controlled household substance until curiosity gives it planetary scale.

### Congo forest — water released from a tree
In one Pygmy-recorded story, Chameleon hears running water inside a tree in a waterless world. Cutting the trunk releases a flood, and the first human pair emerges with the water. Flood and creation are the same event.

### Ababua — the hoarded water
An old woman monopolizes water and kills seekers. Mba defeats her, but her death releases so much water that the world floods; he survives in a treetop. The disaster begins in unjust ownership of a common necessity.

### Bakongo and Lake Dilolo traditions
A disguised, afflicted visitor is refused hospitality except by one household; those hosts leave before rain turns the selfish settlement into a lake. In the Lake Dilolo story, a rejected chieftainess curses a village, which sinks and fills with water. Existing lakes become moral landscapes containing drowned social memory.

### Efik-Ibibio — Sun, Moon and Flood
Sun and Moon invite their friend Flood into a newly enlarged house. Flood repeatedly asks permission to enter farther, bringing fish and water-creatures until the house and earth are submerged. Sun and Moon retreat into the sky. The tale explains celestial distance through excessive hospitality rather than punishment.

### Ekoi — seven water stones
At first earth lacks water. The first man receives seven stones that create springs and later distributes watercourses among descendants. One son plants too many stones together, nearly drowning the world. Water is necessary divine technology made dangerous by greed or incompetence.

### Mandingo charitable survivor
A generous person gives everything away, even feeding an unrecognized deity. He is rewarded and warned to depart before months of rain destroy selfish neighbours. Present humanity descends from the hospitable household.

### Egyptian analogues
Egypt lacks a close canonical ark narrative. In one damaged cosmological text Atum threatens to return creation to primordial water; in the *Destruction of Mankind*, humanity’s destruction is halted when red beer floods the land and pacifies Hathor/Sekhmet. These are watery unmaking or a flood used to prevent annihilation, not equivalents of Noah.

## 6. Australia

Australia offers something importantly different from a universal reset myth: numerous highly local traditions describe country now beneath the sea. Researchers have compared stories from at least 21 coastal locations with postglacial sea-level rise. The interpretation remains an argument rather than mathematical proof, but the geographic specificity is extraordinary.

### Yidindji and the Great Barrier coast
Stories describe ancestors living and moving where reef and sea now lie, with Fitzroy Island attached to the mainland and Green Island much larger. These are maps of lost country rather than ark stories.

### Narrangga — Spencer Gulf
Spencer Gulf was once floodplain country with freshwater lagoons and abundant animals. The sea entered and remained. The remembered former world is ecologically concrete: marsh, hunting and fresh water replaced by gulf.

### Port Phillip Bay
Early records preserve statements that the bay had been kangaroo and opossum hunting ground before inundation. Again, no chosen survivor or moral judgment is required; the subject is changed country.

### Tiwi Islands
An ancestral old woman crawls across former land while water follows, separating Bathurst and Melville islands from the mainland. Movement creates the seascape.

### Rottnest, Carnac and Garden Islands
Tradition records islands once joined to the Western Australian mainland and covered with trees. In one account fire splits the ground and the sea rushes through, severing them.

### Ngarrindjeri — Ngurunderi and Kangaroo Island
Ngurunderi pursues his wives across what was once passable country toward Kangaroo Island; rising water transforms the women into offshore rocks. The narrative binds kinship conflict to the formation of Backstairs Passage.

### Lake Tyers — Tiddalik/frog variants
A giant frog swallows or withholds all water. Animals make it laugh; released water floods the land. In one local version, the flood drowns many beings. It is simultaneously drought story, animal comedy and warning about monopolizing water.

### Murray River and bunyip stories
Some narratives tell of a captured water-being whose enraged parent sends lake water across the country. People fleeing to high ground may become black swans when touched by the flood. The aftermath explains species and sacred danger.

### Arnhem Land and northern coasts
Maung, Gunwinggu, Yolngu/Gumatj and neighbouring records include ancestral beings, ruptured water sources and sea inundation. These stories belong to named custodians and Country; public summaries should not be treated as permission to freely reproduce restricted knowledge.

## 7. Oceania: Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia

### Fiji — Degei/Ndengei
After the killing of the serpent deity Degei’s cherished bird, offenders defend themselves in a fortress. A flood follows; survivors escape in canoes or on high places in different island versions. Some accounts connect surviving groups with chiefly precedence or the spread of canoe and carpentry knowledge.

### Vanuatu/Banks Islands — Qat
Qat builds or prepares a canoe on high ground after receiving warning of a deluge. He, family or companions preserve themselves until water recedes. Versions differ and some published forms show biblical reshaping.

### Palau
In one tradition, disguised gods visiting the world are refused hospitality except by one woman. They warn her before water descends, while the inhospitable settlement is destroyed. In other Palauan cycles, the sea and giant child Uab reshape islands.

### Samoa
One cycle describes primordial Fire and Water fighting until a boundless sea overwhelms the world and Tagaloa recreates land. Another has a survivor, Pili—sometimes human, sometimes lizard—whose union begins later peoples. Fish stranded on mountains become stones, explaining marine forms inland.

### Tuvalu and low-island traditions
Nanumea/Nanumaga and neighbouring atolls preserve storms, inundations and former lands. Because low coral islands can be completely overwashed, these often read as community disaster memory rather than abstract cosmic judgment.

### Ra‘iātea/Tahiti — Ruahatu
Fishers hook the hair of sea deity Ruahatu. Appeased by prayer, he warns a family to take refuge on the small islet Toamarama rather than the mountain they assume will be safe. Ra‘iātea submerges and rises again; survivors establish ritual sites. Knowledge of the deity and reef, not height alone, saves them.

### Tahiti — Pitohiti refuge
A couple reject the obvious highest peak and choose Pitohiti, which remains above the flood. They shelter in a cave, surviving initially on fish and red earth. Birds and insects are preserved by gods. The renewed island begins in hunger, not instant abundance.

### Hawai‘i — Kai a Kahinali‘i and Nu‘u
Traditions describe sea covering the land except Mauna Kea’s summit, where a pair survive. A later Nu‘u version gives the survivor a large vessel with a house; after landing he mistakenly sacrifices to the moon until Kāne descends on a rainbow. Scholars have long noted biblical influence in the vessel narrative while also recording independent Hawaiian inundation traditions.

### Māori — Ruatapu and Paikea
Insulted over status, Ruatapu drowns high-born youths at sea and promises a great wave against the land. Kahutia-te-rangi survives through incantation and whale aid, becoming Paikea. Communities flee to Hikurangi. This is not always a total destruction of humanity; it is ancestry, rank, warning and ocean power.

### Māori — Para-whenua-mea
In a separate recorded cycle, teachers rejected by an impious world build a wide raft with a house and provisions. Ritual calls down rain; a small company survives for months and lands in a transformed Hawaiki, offering seaweed to Tāne, Rangi and other powers. Missionary and recorder influence requires caution.

### Papua New Guinea and New Guinea
Kabadi, Valman, Mamberamo, Samo-Kubo and other traditions feature offended snakes or water beings, local mountain refuges, canoes and the origins of clans. In some, a tiny remnant survives; in others, a flood redistributes people rather than recreating the species.

## 8. North America

There is no single “Native American flood myth.” Hundreds of sovereign peoples maintain distinct traditions. Older English sources often use outdated names and suppress narrators, dates and ceremonial context; community-published versions should be preferred.

### Anishinaabe/Ojibwe — Earth Diver after the flood
After water covers the earth, Nanabozho/Waynaboozhoo floats with surviving animals on a log or raft. Loon, grebe, beaver and others dive for soil; Muskrat succeeds at the cost of life. A tiny pinch of earth is placed on Turtle’s back and grows into the renewed land. Survival depends on the smallest, underestimated helper, and the world is recreated through interspecies council.

### Menominee — Manabush
Manabush attacks underwater powers in revenge; they send pursuing waters. He retreats up a pine or mountain. After the flood, diving animals recover earth. The flood arises from conflict between land and underwater nations, not a deity judging all people.

### Cree and Algonquin variants
Wisakedjak or a related transformer survives with animals and renews land from mud brought up by a diving creature. Local variants differ on why water rises and which animal succeeds. The shared earth-diver pattern is not proof of an identical story.

### Mi’kmaq — Glooscap
Flood traditions associated with Glooscap explain tides, lakes or the aftermath of a world inundation. Some printed forms were filtered through settler retelling; Mi’kmaq-led sources should guide any adaptation.

### Haudenosaunee creation tradition
Sky Woman’s descent to a watery world and animals building land on Turtle is a creation from primordial water, not necessarily a “great flood” destroying an earlier humanity. It is often misclassified in flood lists.

### Caddo — the growing reed
A voice instructs a man to plant a hollow reed. It grows rapidly; a family and “good” animals enter while waters rise around sky-touching monsters. Turtle undermines the monsters, water subsides and wind dries the earth. A plant rather than a built ark bridges worlds.

### Mandan — Lone Man and the flood
Mandan ceremonial memory includes a great flood, a survivor associated with a large vessel and the First Man/Lone Man complex. The Okipa ceremony historically renewed the people’s covenant with survival. Early European records and biblical comparisons must not replace Mandan interpretation.

### Lakota — Unktehi and the flood
Water monsters or Unktehi unleash a flood in a conflict involving Thunderbird powers. Survivors reach a mountain or are protected; the narrative establishes a renewed relationship among powers. Details vary by keeper and should not be flattened into an ark tale.

### Diné/Navajo — emergence through water worlds
Holy beings and First People ascend through successive worlds. In one stage, Water Monster’s flood rises after a taboo or offence; people escape through a giant reed into the next world. This is vertical emergence and moral learning, not a boat-deluge.

### Jicarilla Apache
A flood may be survived on a mountain or in a sealed vessel coated against water; animal scouts and the renewed earth appear in recorded versions. Distinct Apache peoples hold distinct emergence accounts.

### Hopi — world transitions
In one cycle, Spider Grandmother guides worthy people into hollow reeds as a corrupted world is destroyed by water; they later travel by reed boats to the next world. Other Hopi cycles emphasize destruction by fire or ice. These are sacred accounts of successive worlds, not simply regional versions of Genesis.

### Tohono O’odham/Papago and Akimel O’odham/Pima
Creator and culture heroes survive destructive water in vessels, jars or on mountains; rivalry among creators continues after the flood and shapes present land. Published versions vary sharply and older tribal names should be replaced where communities do so.

### Pacific Northwest coastal peoples
Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka’wakw, Nuu-chah-nulth/Makah, Klallam, Skokomish, Squamish and others preserve distinct high-water, tsunami, canoe and mountain-anchor traditions. Typical local details include canoes tied to peaks with cedar ropes, survivors stranded on mountaintops, animals transformed, and villages relocated. Some likely encode earthquake-tsunami experience, but each account requires local historical and cultural context.

### California and Great Basin traditions
Miwok, Maidu, Wintu, Pomo, Ohlone, Yurok, Shasta, Salinan, Yuma/Quechan, Havasupai and many neighbours have recorded inundation stories involving tule rafts, mountain refuges, creator animals and earth-divers. They do not form one Californian narrative.

### Arctic traditions
Inuit and Yupik accounts collected around Alaska, Canada and Greenland include giant waves, overturned seas, kayak or umiak survivors, and waters associated with the movement of earth or sea spirits. Some “Noah and mammoth” versions clearly display missionary influence; local-language provenance is essential.

## 9. Mesoamerica and the Caribbean

### Mexica/Aztec — the Sun of Water
In the cycle of successive Suns, an earlier world ends in water. In one widely reported form, Nata and Nena survive inside a cypress or hollow tree after warning from Titlacahuan/Tezcatlipoca; breaking instructions afterward leads to transformation into dogs. Other sources say the inhabitants become fish. The flood is one of several cosmic endings, alongside wind, fire and jaguars.

### Nahua variants
Different Nahua communities preserve local survivor names, trees, gourds and animal transformations. “The Aztec flood myth” is therefore shorthand for a textual family, not one fixed narrative.

### K’iche’ Maya — wooden people in the *Popol Vuh*
Creator gods first attempt animals, mud people and wooden people. The wooden beings can speak and multiply but lack hearts and do not remember their makers. A flood of resin destroys them while neglected dogs, grinding stones, pots and hearths turn against their owners. Survivors become monkeys. The pre-flood world fails because humans do not reciprocate with gods, animals or tools.

### Maya and Chol variants
In related but distinct traditions, survivors in high trees may be transformed into monkeys or punished after the deluge. Colonial destruction of Maya books and later Christian pressure complicate the textual record.

### Purépecha/Tarascan — Tespi
Tespi is warned of a flood and enters a vessel with family, seeds and animals. Birds test the receding water; a vulture stays to feed on corpses and receives its scavenger fate. The story was recorded after Spanish contact and may carry biblical features.

### Wixárika/Huichol — Watákame
A man repeatedly clears a field only to find trees restored overnight. An old woman/deity warns him and directs him to build a vessel, preserving maize and other life. He survives with a female companion, and the renewed world is ritually ordered. Maize and agricultural obligation, not merely animal pairs, are central.

### Cora and Tepecano
Close regional relatives of the Watákame cycle preserve a warned cultivator, dog or old woman helper, vessel or hollow tree, birds and renewed agriculture. Variants must be credited separately.

### Mixtec, Zapotec and Trique traditions
Recorded stories include flood survivors in trees, caves or vessels; founding couples; and transformations into monkeys or birds. Some appear in colonial documents, others in later oral collections.

### Caribbean/Kalinago traditions
Island traditions include destructive seas, culture heroes and people saved on mountains or in canoes. Colonial population collapse and outside retelling make careful sourcing especially important.

## 10. South America

### Andes — Viracocha and Unu Pachakuti
Viracocha’s first beings—often described as giants or stone people in an age of darkness—prove unruly. A great flood destroys or petrifies them, leaving a few survivors. At Lake Titicaca, Viracocha brings out sun, moon and stars and fashions or disperses renewed peoples from stones, caves, springs and sacred places. Flood, petrification and ethnic origins belong to one re-creation.

### Ancasmarca — the warning llamas
Llamas refuse food and watch the stars. They warn a shepherd that a celestial conjunction foretells flood. The shepherd, family and animals climb Ancasmarca; water reaches the summit before subsiding. The llamas’ foresight explains their intimate status in highland life.

### Cañari — brothers on Huacayñán
Two brothers survive on a mountain that rises with the water. Afterward, mysterious food appears while they are away; they discover macaw women bringing it. One brother joins a macaw woman, and their descendants renew the Cañari. Survival requires marriage with a nonhuman mountain being.

### Muisca — Bochica drains the waters
The Chibcha/Muisca world is flooded, often through the water power Chibchacum or Huitaca in colonial accounts. Culture hero Bochica breaks open the rock at Tequendama, making the great waterfall and draining the Bogotá savanna. Like Yu, he saves a civilization through hydraulic transformation rather than an ark.

### Mapuche — Trentren Vilu and Caicai Vilu
Sea-serpent Caicai raises the waters while earth-serpent Trentren raises land to protect people and animals. Survivors climb the growing hills; some become fish or sea mammals, others are burned by the sun when lifted too high. The present coast records an unresolved contest between sea and land.

### Guaraní — the great water
Various Guaraní traditions tell of a coming or past deluge, a culture hero who escapes by climbing a palm or entering a vessel, and the search for the Land Without Evil. These motifs vary widely across Guaraní-speaking peoples and centuries.

### Tupi/Tupinambá — Monan and Irin-Magé
Colonial sources record an old world destroyed by fire and then flood, or a culture hero causing waters to rise. Tamandaré and family survive in a palm tree in some versions and renew humanity. Contact-era names and Christian editing require caution.

### Karajá/Carajá
Hunters pursue animals into a burrow or violate a boundary associated with underwater beings; waters rise, and survivors cling to high places or trees. The catastrophe emerges from crossing species worlds.

### Yanomami
In some cycles, Moon’s blood falls to earth and becomes violent forest peoples; other water catastrophes follow broken kinship or spirit relations. These complex living cosmologies should not be reduced to a “Yanomami Noah.”

### Warao and Orinoco traditions
Warao, Tamanaku, Ye’kwana/Makiritare and neighbouring peoples preserve several different high-water and world-renewal cycles involving sacred trees, canoes, twins, celestial waters and the origin of rivers. The Orinoco is not background but a living cosmological axis.

### Selk’nam and Yámana/Yaghan
Tierra del Fuego traditions include the sea rising through Moon’s power or primordial social conflict, survivors on high ground, and landscapes formed as water recedes. They emerge from an archipelago where canoe travel and sudden weather are central to life.

## 11. Recurring patterns—and important differences

### What the former world is like
- **Already civilized:** Mesopotamian cities, crafts and kingship; Berossus’s archives.
- **A golden age:** Yima’s deathless abundance; Romani rivers of milk and wine; later Jewish antediluvian ease.
- **A failed draft of humanity:** Maya wooden people; Andean giants or stone beings.
- **Crowded or noisy:** Atrahasis and Yima, where success itself destabilizes the world.
- **Ordinary named country:** Australian and Pacific coastal stories, where familiar hunting grounds, reefs and villages are submerged.
- **A world with different physics:** ocean in a pot, water inside a tree, earth resting on an animal, sky held by pillars.

### Why water rises
- Moral violence or corruption: Noah, Deucalion, some Tumbainot accounts.
- Noise and overpopulation: Atrahasis.
- Failed reciprocity: *Popol Vuh*, Bakongo hospitality stories, Huichol agricultural cycle.
- Broken taboo or injured water-being: Ruahatu, Degei, many island traditions.
- Cosmic instability: Ymir, Nüwa, Tuvinian earth-supporter.
- Political/ecological failure: Gun and Yu.
- No moral cause at all: many Australian sea-level memories and local tsunami traditions.

### What must survive
Not every tradition saves breeding pairs of animals. Survivors carry:
- **Seeds and food plants** — later Manu, Huichol, many Asian and African vessels.
- **Craftspeople and written knowledge** — Uta-napishtim and Xisuthrus.
- **Fire** — Andaman and many island traditions.
- **Ritual speech** — Polynesian rafts, Yima’s enclosure, Andean re-creation.
- **Animal relationships** — Earth-diver councils, Hailibu’s bird warning, Cañari macaws.
- **Memory of place** — Australian coastlines and drowned-city legends.

### What the second world gains—and loses
- Mortality, infertility and population limits in Atrahasis.
- Meat eating and covenant law in Genesis.
- Agriculture and hydraulic government under Yu.
- New languages and dispersed peoples in Siberian and many other variants.
- Present animal forms: monkeys, scavenging birds, fish, stone animals and black swans.
- Present geography: islands, gorges, lakes, waterfalls, reefs and salt seas.

## 12. Responsible use in Aethel

1. **Do not combine everything into “proof” of one flood.** Similar stories can arise from river flooding, tsunamis, postglacial sea rise, shared literary ancestry, cultural diffusion and universal narrative concerns.
2. **Separate textual families.** Ziusudra, Atrahasis, Uta-napishtim and Noah are related Near Eastern traditions, not four independent witnesses.
3. **Mark contact influence.** Hawaiian Nu‘u, Tumbainot and some Siberian ark stories were recorded after Christian contact and may blend layers.
4. **Prefer specific places.** “The sea covered the world” often means the narrator’s inhabited world. A drowned marsh, reef passage or named mountain can feel more authentic than a featureless globe.
5. **Respect living custodians.** Indigenous stories are not an unrestricted motif warehouse. Use public, community-authorized accounts; avoid ceremonial or restricted material; credit the people and source.
6. **Let differences remain.** Yu channels water; Muskrat brings earth; Nüwa repairs the sky; Manu follows a fish; the Yidindji remember a coastline. Their value lies in difference, not forced sameness.

### Scope note
The broad legacy index used for discovery names traditions from more than 200 cultural or regional groupings across Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia, Oceania and the Americas. Inclusion in such an index does **not** establish independence, antiquity, global scale or immunity from outside influence. This compendium therefore presents representative narrative families in detail while acknowledging many additional local variants that require community-specific research.
